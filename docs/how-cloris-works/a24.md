# How Cloris Works

*An overview written for A24's talent leadership. Six chapters; about half an hour to read.*

---

## Chapter 1 — The Brief

Watch a senior recruiter open a new search. For a few minutes they do something quiet — they translate. The job description goes in; what comes out is a different object: the criteria they'll actually use to evaluate a candidate for this role at this customer. Which companies count as frontier this quarter. What "senior" means here specifically. The four patterns that look like a fit and aren't. The two candidates from last month who taught the team what to watch for. The translation is most of the work, and almost no software stores it. Cloris's brief is built to hold it.

A brief in Cloris is not a job description. It is a structured object — a JSON file with named sections — that carries the parts of role judgment a senior recruiter actually uses: capability areas (what the person must be able to do), a depth distinction (the difference between someone who builds in this space and someone who consumes its outputs — a distinction every senior recruiter has, and most tools cannot represent), non-fit patterns with examples and reasons, employer signal rules (when "worked at X" is enough evidence, when it isn't), and a small set of calibration candidates: real people from past searches, labeled by your team as strong saves, wrong saves, or borderline. There is also a quieter field called *facial calibration* — the fraction of profiles a recruiter would expect this brief to say yes to. The number sounds boring; it does enormous work. It's how the system notices when it's drifting. When the brief's prediction and the run's outcome stop agreeing, Cloris asks for revision rather than charging through.

Two architectural choices in the brief bear directly on the kind of trust a tool like this has to earn on a creative team. The first is that every revision is a new file — `brief-creative-recruiter-v1`, `-v1.4`, and so on — and Cloris is intolerant of ambiguity at run time. If two siblings tie at the same version, or any sibling lacks a parseable version suffix, the run stops and asks the operator to choose (`shared/recruiter_brief_resolution.py:74-92`). The cost is occasional friction. The benefit is that no run ever quietly executes against a stale or wrong brief — the kind of silent drift that turns a useful tool into a confusing one over a quarter.

The second is that revisions are clamped. When Cloris proposes how the brief should change after a run, based on what your recruiter saved, rejected, or flagged, the calibration thresholds can move by at most 0.10 in a single revision (`shared/brief_iteration.py:71`). The role gets sharper over weeks of feedback, never in one motion. A single bad run cannot reshape the role overnight. The recruiter's accumulated judgment is treated as the durable thing; the LLM's proposed revision is a draft that bends the role, not a verdict that overwrites it.

What this gives a recruiting team in practice is that the role definition lives in one place, gets sharper over time, and is the same object that the discovery layer, the evaluation layer, and the reconciliation step all read from. There is no silent translation between "what we told the system" and "what the system is doing." If a candidate is surfaced and your recruiter disagrees, the disagreement points at a specific section of a specific version of a specific brief — and the next revision reflects the resolution.

The brief is also where Cloris learns your team's taste. Calibration examples are not generic; they are real candidates A24's recruiters have labeled. Over weeks, the brief becomes a written record of what your team means by *right for this role* — something no off-the-shelf sourcing tool produces, because no off-the-shelf sourcing tool stores the judgment in the first place.

Everything else in Cloris — the search, the evaluation, the reconciliation, the candidate workspace — is downstream of this one object. It is not a control panel for finding candidates. It is the working memory of a recruiter, made executable.

## Chapter 2 — The Search

The space of plausible candidates for any senior role is larger than any human, or any reasonable team of humans, can read. Most tools paper over this with keyword search and rank-by-relevance: type a few terms, get a few hundred matches, hope the right person is in the list. For volume hiring, that is fine. For the kind of role A24 actually invests in finding the right person for, it is wrong twice. It pulls in candidates whose profiles match the words but not the role. And it misses the people who do not describe themselves in the language of the job description — which is most of the people who would actually be a good hire.

Cloris does not search in that sense. It plans a finite set of queries from the brief, executes them, and stops on principled grounds, recording why.

Each platform Cloris searches is its own pipeline, because each is a fundamentally different problem. The LinkedIn pipeline (`linkedin/orchestrator.py`) drives a real recruiter session — Recruiter Search, Recruiter Projects, profile pages — and includes the apparatus required for that to work over hours: humanized timing, decoy interactions, a search-intelligence layer that tracks page insights and search variants and assesses drift between expected and actual results (`linkedin/search_intelligence.py`). The GitHub pipeline (`github/orchestrator.py`) is API-only — repository searches, contribution graphs, organization membership, social-graph traversal — and has no browser, no human-mimicry layer, and a different governance model. Cloris is not one search tool with two adapters. It is a small set of source-specific orchestrators that share a substrate.

What they share is the rest of the candidate's life inside the system. Every candidate, regardless of source, moves through the same lifecycle state machine: `discovered → snippet_extracted → facial_started → facial_terminal → full_started → full_terminal` (`shared/runtime_state/store.py:62-71`). Light evidence first; a quick triage decision; deep evidence; a final decision with named rationale. The store enforces transitions. A candidate cannot skip a step. Every decision is recorded.

There is one architectural choice here worth flagging because it shapes how a multi-source recruiting picture composes. When the same person appears on LinkedIn and GitHub, Cloris stores them as two rows in the candidate table, not one. The unique constraint is `UNIQUE(brief_id, source, identity_key)` (`shared/runtime_state/store.py:182`). Cross-source identity resolution — the layer that recognizes a GitHub username and a LinkedIn profile as the same human — is its own module (`shared/cross_module_identity/`). The reason the architecture is set up this way is that the world has more than two recruiting-relevant sources: published research (OpenAlex, PubMed), patents (USPTO), open-source maintainers (npm, PyPI), creative portfolios (Behance), defense-related publications, and so on. Cloris is built so that adding a new source is the work of writing one new orchestrator and one new identity adapter, not rebuilding the storage layer. For A24 specifically, this is what makes "Cloris for creative-technical roles, with a portfolio-aware discovery layer alongside LinkedIn" a real near-term product, not a long architectural project.

And the searches are bounded. Both pipelines plan a finite list of queries before issuing any of them, execute them, and stop with one of a small set of recorded reasons: `score_above_threshold`, `high_confidence_match`, `no_new_urls`, `single_surface_name_variant_stop`, `plan_exhausted` (`shared/recruiter_identity_schemas.py:170-178`). When a run finishes, the operator can see exactly why each search ended. This is unusual. Most search tools are a black box: they ran, they returned what they returned, the operator has no idea whether they ran out of useful queries or simply stopped.

What this gives a recruiting team is the ability to trust the run's recall claim. When Cloris reports "this is who showed up on LinkedIn and GitHub for this brief, and these are the searches we ran, and these are the principled reasons each search stopped," the report is auditable. A skeptical recruiter can ask "did you try X variant?" and the answer is in the planned-queries record.

## Chapter 3 — The Judgment

A candidate has surfaced. The harder question begins.

The naive answer is a relevance score: an AI looks at a profile and produces a number. Cloris does not work that way, and the reason matters. A score does not decompose. Asked whether a candidate is at the right level, doing the kind of work that actually counts, deeply enough to translate, with experience that transfers — those are four separate questions, and a senior recruiter answers them separately. A score-style AI flattens them into a vibes rating and hopes.

Cloris's evaluation is structural. Every candidate, on every platform, runs through the same four-step procedure: capability mapping, depth test, transferability test, decision. The procedure is implemented in `shared/judger.py`; the per-platform judgment templates (`linkedin/judgment_templates.py`, `github/judgment_templates.py`) only frame the evidence — what a LinkedIn snippet looks like, what a GitHub portfolio looks like — and inherit the procedure from the shared layer. The four-step is paradigm-neutral by design. It works for ML researchers, defense engineers, biotech computational scientists, and creative-technical roles without structural change. The role-specific calibration comes from the brief: capability areas, depth distinction, calibration examples, employer signal rules. Code does not embed role-specific vocabulary; it composes brief-supplied vocabulary into the procedure.

The evaluation runs in two stages, with different costs and stakes. *Facial* is a quick triage on the surface evidence (the LinkedIn card, the GitHub bio): is this profile worth opening? Output is `FACIAL_YES`, `FACIAL_NO`, or `FACIAL_BORDERLINE` (`shared/judger.py:38`). A yes routes to *full* evaluation, where Cloris opens the profile, builds a structured summary, and runs the four-step procedure to a final decision. The final decision is `SAVE`, `REJECT`, or one of three nuanced save modes — `INFERENTIAL_SAVE`, `TRANSFERABLE_SAVE`, `SIGNAL_SAVE` (`shared/bias_controls.py:53-58`). The nuanced modes are how the system records *why* a save is a save: `TRANSFERABLE_SAVE` explicitly says "this person is in an adjacent specialty and the experience translates," with the rationale captured in the candidate record. A recruiter reviewing saves a week later does not have to reverse-engineer Cloris's reasoning.

What makes the autonomous loop trustable is not just that the system judges well. It is that the system watches itself, and stops when it suspects it is judging badly. The bias monitor (`shared/bias_controls.py`) records every decision the system makes and, after each search string and each page of results, runs a set of checks: too many consecutive saves on one query, too many consecutive rejects, save-rate spikes, parse-failure rates above tolerance, facial-rate anomalies against the brief's facial calibration. When a check fires, the monitor produces an Alert. Alerts have severity. Some are `flag` — log and continue. Some are `pause` — *stop the current search, surface to the operator*. The most striking of these is `CONSECUTIVE_SAVES`, whose code comment names the failure mode the alert exists to catch: *"Volume-threshold inversion — the Session 5 failure mode where the agent saves everyone because the population 'looks' relevant as a group"* (`shared/bias_controls.py:175-180`). That kind of compounding error is exactly what an autonomous evaluation system has to be defended against. The defense is built in.

The bias monitor is also brief-aware. Its thresholds — how many consecutive saves before pausing, what facial-yes rate is anomalous, how the save-rate spike window is sized — come from the brief's `bias_controls` and `facial_calibration` sections (`shared/bias_controls.py:119-130`). A different role, with a different expected hit rate, gets different thresholds. The same code; different calibration. This is the architectural pattern that lets one evaluation substrate work across the kinds of roles A24 actually hires for.

## Chapter 4 — The Run

An autonomous tool earns its trust at the boundary where things go wrong. Networks fail. Sessions disconnect. Laptops sleep. A recruiter starts a run on Tuesday morning, has to leave for an off-site at 11 AM, and wants the run to keep going, then to be exactly where they expect when they reopen the laptop on Wednesday. The plumbing under that promise is the part of Cloris that took the most engineering, and it is the architectural choice that makes everything else in the product possible.

Every Cloris run writes its canonical state to a SQLite database at `runtime_state.sqlite3`, one per state directory (`shared/runtime_state/store.py`). The schema is small but load-bearing: `runs` (the actual run, with start time, status, stop reason, and the brief snapshot used at launch), `work_units` (each search query the system is working through), `candidates` (each person discovered), `candidate_attempts` (each attempt to evaluate a candidate, with stage and status), `events` (an append-only log of everything that happened), `side_effects` (irreversible actions the system has taken — the LinkedIn save click, the outreach email — with idempotency keys so they never fire twice). Every state change passes through this database. Every transition between lifecycle states is checked against an `ALLOWED_LIFECYCLE_TRANSITIONS` graph (`shared/runtime_state/store.py:62-71`); the store rejects an illegal transition rather than silently allowing it.

The other files you see in a run output — `progress.json`, `snippets.jsonl`, `final_judgments.jsonl`, and so on — are not control state. They are *projections* rebuilt from the canonical store. Code that needs to make a runtime decision is forbidden from reading them. This rule is enforced by architecture review and is consequential: projections drift over time as code evolves, while canonical state is durable. A bug in a projection cannot corrupt a run; it can only produce a misleading file that gets regenerated on the next checkpoint.

There is exactly one writer per state directory: the detached worker process that is actually executing the run (`cloris/worker.py`). The Cloris UI opens the same database in *read-only* mode (`cloris/control_plane.py:8-17`) — `sqlite3.connect(f"file:{path}?mode=ro", uri=True)`. The UI cannot corrupt the run by accident; it can only watch. A `RuntimeStateLock` enforces the one-writer contract at the filesystem level. A second worker attempting to operate in the same state directory is rejected.

This setup gives stop, resume, and crash recovery effectively for free. Stop a run mid-search? The canonical state is on disk. Resume tomorrow? The next worker reads the lifecycle column, sees what is and is not done, and picks up where the state machine left off. Crash? The events table is append-only; the projection layer reconstructs progress from it. Close the laptop? The detached worker outlives the UI window; the worker writes a `worker.json` sidecar that the UI rediscovers on reopen. The recruiter does not have to think about any of this. They close the laptop and reopen it.

One specific property of the run state matters for trust: the brief itself is pinned at run start. The orchestrator writes `brief_path_at_launch`, `brief_content_hash`, and `brief_snapshot_json` into the runs row (`shared/runtime_state/store.py:303-317`). Six weeks later, when a recruiter is reviewing the candidates the system saved, they can answer the question "what did the brief look like when Cloris decided to save this person?" with the exact JSON — including the brief's full calibration vocabulary, the non-fit patterns, the calibration examples used at the time. This is the foundation under the closed feedback loop in chapter 5, and it is also why drift between an on-disk brief and its used-by-this-run snapshot is detectable and surfaced (`cloris/control_plane.py:_detect_brief_drift`).

Schema migrations are gated and idempotent (`shared/runtime_state/store.py:_migrate`). The schema has evolved seven times; legacy data survives every migration. Old runs from earlier in the project still open and inspect cleanly. This is the discipline that lets a tool sold to a creative team for years not break the work it has already produced.

The runtime substrate is not the part of the product anyone notices. It is the part that makes Cloris not embarrass itself in the third week of a customer's use, when the romance of a new tool is over and what is left is whether the thing actually behaves like infrastructure.

## Chapter 5 — The Learning

Most recruiting software is a one-shot. You run a search, you get results, you start over for the next role. Whatever you learned in the run — that two specific employers produce far better candidates than their reputation suggests, that a particular skill keyword pulls in adjacent-specialty noise, that a specific calibration example was sharper than expected — disappears the moment the run ends. The next search starts from zero.

Cloris is built on the opposite premise. A great recruiting team gets sharper with each search they do, and the tool's job is to make that sharpening compound rather than evaporate.

Two layers compound. The first is the brief itself. After every run, Cloris processes what happened — every save, every reject, the rationale attached to each decision, recruiter feedback on misses and incorrect saves — and proposes a brief revision (`shared/brief_iteration.py`). The proposal is bounded (calibration thresholds clamp at 0.10 per revision, from chapter 1) and produces a new brief version with a tracked rationale. Your recruiter reviews the proposal, edits or accepts, and the next run launches against the revised brief. Over weeks, the brief is no longer a generic role definition; it is A24's version of that role, calibrated against your team's taste, refined against your past mistakes. This is what "the tool gets smarter with use" actually means at the data layer: the brief that defines what the system is searching for is the artifact that learns.

The second layer is market intelligence. Each run writes structured evidence to a per-market artifact (`market_intelligence/engine.py`): `output/market_intelligence/<market_key>/market-intel.json`, where `market_key` is a hash of role title, geography, and role level (`market_intelligence/engine.py:181-188`). Multiple briefs in the same role/geography/level share a market — a "creative-technical lead in NYC" brief for one project and another for a different project ingest into the same market artifact. The artifact accumulates: market hypotheses, talent-pool patterns, sourcing implications, employer signal evidence, research opportunities the team has not yet explored. The synthesis goes through a multi-stage agent pipeline — planner, critic, internal-synthesis backend (`market_intelligence/agent_backends.py`) — so the artifact is not a dump of every run, it is an interpreted view of the market that grows as evidence accumulates.

The market layer is what powers "Learn about the market" — one of the five locked verbs on Cloris's home screen, sitting at the same level as "Start a search" and "Read a run's report." It is not a settings panel. It is a primary action. Asking *what is going on in this hiring market right now* is something every senior recruiter does informally, in their head, by reading a lot of profiles and forming an unstated thesis. Cloris does it explicitly, persists the thesis, and feeds it back into the next brief revision.

The full closed loop, end to end:

1. The brief is authored, or iterated from a previous version.
2. The run executes against the platforms the brief targets; the runtime state captures what happened in detail.
3. Recruiters review saves and rejects, mark them, add feedback.
4. Brief iteration proposes a revision based on the feedback and the run's outcome.
5. Market intelligence ingests the run's evidence into the per-market artifact.
6. The next brief revision sees both the recruiter's feedback *and* the updated market context.
7. The next run uses the revised brief, against an updated picture of the market.

This loop is the actual product. The search, the evaluation, the runtime state — those are infrastructure for the loop. A version of Cloris that did not close this loop would be a one-shot search tool. The compounding is the point.

For a team like A24's, the practical implication is this: the work of teaching Cloris what your team means by "right for this role" is not lost when the recruiter who taught it changes seats. It is captured in the briefs and in the market artifacts. New recruiters joining the team inherit the calibrated tool. The taste survives the staffing.

## Chapter 6 — The Surface

How the screen looks is part of the same conviction.

Cloris does not look like SaaS chrome. There is no dashboard of metrics, no header full of navigation, no bright "AI-powered" branding, no tooltips that explain what every number means. The home screen is a calm horizontal layout of recent runs and a small set of named actions: write a brief, start a search, resume a search, read a run's report, learn about the market. The launch is one input field and a button. The run-review surface is built around the candidate, not the diagnostics.

The design rules are codified — `docs/cloris-surface-design-rules.md` enumerates 18 binding editorial rules that ship-block any new surface. There is a voice running through the product. It is sparse. It does not appear inside focused work; it gets out of the way during candidate review. It appears at transitions: launching a run, finishing one, recovering from a stop. The voice has a specific shape — Cloris is a person who finds her glasses, not a brand mascot who reminds you to drink water — and it lives in the same git repo as the code.

This is not aesthetic decoration on top of the system. It is the recognition that the recruiter using Cloris has taste, that the tool will be looked at for two hours a day for months on end, and that earning the recruiter's affection over that span is part of the job. The substrate, the brief, the closed loop are the moat. The surface is what makes the recruiter actually want to keep using the tool long enough for the loop to compound.

That is the whole tour. A brief that is a structured object, not a search query. A search that plans, executes, and stops with reasons. A judgment that decomposes into capability, depth, transferability, and a defended decision — watched over by a bias monitor that can pause the run. A runtime substrate built so the work survives any boundary where things go wrong. A learning loop that turns each run into a sharper brief and a richer view of the market. A surface that reflects the seriousness of the underlying work and makes a recruiter want to stay.

One thesis underneath all of it: *do the boring part of sourcing in the background so the recruiter can do the judgment part*. It is the only sentence in this document I wrote first and have not changed since.
